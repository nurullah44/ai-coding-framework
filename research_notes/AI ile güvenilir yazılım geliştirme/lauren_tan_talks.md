## 2. What does she actually say? Full-source reading notes and timestamped quotations

### Takeaway
In T, trust depends on an environment that makes correct behavior observable and bad patterns difficult to create. She explicitly prioritizes codebase structure and static enforcement over guidance and human review, and gives application/process-boundary examples rather than just an analogy. [T, 14:56–18:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=896s); [T, 26:42–29:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1602s).

### Cited Findings

#### T — standalone talk, published 2026-09-21; full transcript read, 00:01–38:00

- **Thesis / trust before parallelism.** At 00:34–00:55 she argues that a well-prepared agent environment can produce high-quality code faster. At 05:29–06:42, the initial state is one to five agents requiring constant correction; spawning a hundred before trusting their work scales regressions. At 04:24–05:08 she says PR volume was not her original goal: “That was not a goal of mine at all.” [T, 04:24–06:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=264s).
- **Verification origin: replace a manual observation bottleneck.** At 02:28–04:13 she describes performance problems in Cursor's Agents Window, manually capturing Chrome performance traces and heap snapshots, and asking whether an agent could run the application, locate hot spots and hill-climb performance itself. This is a concrete performance workflow, not evidence that all forms of verification are solved. [T, 02:28–04:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=148s).
- **Empirical verification versus formal proof.** At 07:06–08:36 she distinguishes teaching agents to run/debug apps from formal verification, mentioning Lean and TLA+ and business invariants. She calls formal verification “still very much an open question”. No worked Lean/TLA+ example follows. [T, 07:06–08:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=426s).
- **Reusable control CLI.** At 08:38–10:13, control-glass uses Chrome DevTools Protocol. Her objective is to “collect traces and collect evidence”, including whether the performance bar is met; she recommends a maintained CLI in the skill directory so different sessions do not invent different scripts. [T, 08:38–10:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=518s).
- **Feature map as materialized memory.** At 10:16–12:31, a tiny screenshot with three question marks is not enough context, even for an agent that can launch the app. The map explains available features, what each does, how users reach it, keyboard shortcuts and DOM elements; it lives with the skill in the codebase and an automation maintains it. She calls it a “form of materialized memory”. CLI plus map became “critical infrastructure for our team”. [T, 10:16–12:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=616s).
- **Correctness is not the whole quality bar.** “verification is really about correctness.” At 12:33–13:16 she uses a checkout-button example: does it actually do the requested action? She says observing functionality alone does not say much about code quality/performance; at 13:20–14:52 she adds engineering playbooks and real metrics/telemetry. This does not contradict collecting performance traces: it distinguishes a functional observation from broader quality evidence. [T, 12:33–14:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=753s).
- **Codebase as agents' memory / nearest-pattern extension.** At 15:45–15:55: “the code base is really like the best form of memory because agents love to extend existing patterns that they see.” At 16:18–16:28 she says agents will generally extend the existing code rather than refactor it on every PR. At 20:31–20:44 she repeats that the codebase is memory because they extend its patterns. [T, 15:45–16:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=945s); [T, 20:31–20:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1231s).
- **Enforcement ladder.** At 14:56–18:55 and 36:23–37:06: architecture/data structures that make the mistake impossible first; static analysis (linters, compiler diagnostics, CI) next; rules/Bugbot/skills layered over that; human-enforced style guides last. Rules may not be read, and users may ignore them. The structural aim is that a repeated error “becomes categorically impossible.” [T, 14:56–18:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=896s); [T, 36:23–37:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=2183s).
- **Design the easy path to be correct.** At 19:29–20:30: “agents love taking shortcuts.” She proposes a framework where the shortcut is the right path, even if the restrictions annoy humans. Its contributors may include PMs, designers and CEOs piloting agents with little context. [T, 19:29–20:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1169s).
- **Bad patterns spread.** At 20:44–21:55 she warns existing anti-patterns “will spread kind of like a virus”: a small workaround or a comment justifying one gets copied, and within days or weeks becomes the default pattern. This is her experienced-based warning, not an independently measured rate. [T, 20:44–21:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1244s).
- **The comment example / a stated change in her view.** At 22:53–24:26 she says comments initially seemed permissible because humans use them for tricky cases. She then saw agents use them to justify not fixing the actual problem, covering it with a “band-aid or a short-term solution”. She says Dune bans comments for that reason. This spoken statement does not specify the exceptions that may exist in current written policy. [T, 22:53–24:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1373s).
- **Gardener / stop propagation before completing cleanup.** At 24:28–26:41: every team needs a gardener to spot unwanted growth early; delete existing tech debt, establish one conventional path, and add a lint rule against new bad patterns immediately. “you can at least stop the bleeding”; cleanup may follow later. The target is code she would be happy for the next agent to copy. [T, 24:28–26:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1468s).
- **Application architecture, with examples.** At 26:42–27:45 she describes Dune as the client framework for Grok Bot: features co-located in one folder, a React entry point comparable to a route, transcript cards, a host on the bot VM and a client. At 27:45–29:15 she gives a process/import-boundary example: Electron main-process code must not accidentally enter the renderer, as slow imports caused Agents Window performance problems. She names 16 ms for 60 fps and 8 ms for 120 fps as UI work budgets and says Dune enforces separation through the import/dependency graph. This is architectural structure and UI performance; it is not a security/network architecture walkthrough. [T, 26:42–29:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1602s).
- **Capture expert judgment in the framework.** At 29:24–30:36, tribal knowledge previously expressed in review comments should move into the framework/codebase; the code is the materialized state the next agent extends. [T, 29:24–30:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1764s).
- **Small / low-context models.** At 31:21–31:36: “even a agent with not a lot of reasoning can come in and and actually write code that's good”. At 32:20–32:31 the question is how agents with little knowledge can still do good work. This supports the environment-for-weaker-agents thesis; it is not a benchmark or a recommendation for Luna/Haiku by name. [T, 31:21–32:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1881s).
- **Outer-loop orchestration.** At 32:34–34:39 she describes bots aggregating Slack, Datadog, Sentry and PlanetScale information, listening to routines/events and starting cloud agents; she does not think a sophisticated “company brain” is necessary. At 34:42–35:12 she reports automations reproducing bugs and opening PRs. [T, 32:34–35:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1954s).
- **Kitchen analogy, exact caption wording and extent.** At 00:56–01:05: “I'm not a fan of the term software factory. I like the analogy of a Michelin kitchen better”. At 01:31–01:38: “we're still responsible for the final outcome and thinking about our kitchen setup”. She contrasts creative product work with assembly-line production, then names line cooks, “sue chefs” (ASR spelling), equipment, training, dishwashers and the ratio of cooks to dishwashers (01:05–02:02). At 31:39–32:20 she makes the analogy operational: tools/skills train workers; the kitchen layout makes correct work easy; if a cook or dishwasher keeps tripping over something, remove the cause so others do not trip. She returns to the kitchen at 30:38, 33:38–33:51 and the close. **Detail given:** staffing, training, equipment, layout, correcting recurring hazards, and human responsibility for the assembled result. **Detail not given:** numerical staffing ratios, a ticket flow, PM/reviewer/QA mapping, or a precise kitchen station protocol. [T, 00:56–02:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=56s); [T, 31:39–32:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=1899s); [T, 33:38–33:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z-jNqqIYGm4&t=2018s).

Do not turn this into independently verified throughput or silently select one count.
